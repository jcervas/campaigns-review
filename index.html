    Chapter 1 in one slide

    The top line is not the election

    THE 2024 PUZZLE

    Down the ballot, no wave

    Trump won the electoral college and the popular vote. Republicans won the Senate but lost two House seats. No governorship changed hands, and only the Michigan House flipped.

    THE FRAMEWORK

    Rules, reality, strategy, choice

    Rules govern every actor. Reality is the context candidates cannot control. Strategy is the part they can. Citizens choose whether and how to vote.

    THE STANDARDS

    Free choice, equality, deliberation, free speech

    The standards for judging elections sometimes conflict. Free speech, as the Court read it in Buckley v. Valeo, limits campaign finance reforms meant to promote equality.

    THE STAKES

    Democratic backsliding

    Leaders can degrade democracy to keep power without banning elections. The aftermath of 2020 showed that democratic elections cannot be taken for granted.

    48%

    of 2024 U.S. House primaries had at least two candidates. With one name on the ballot, there is no choice to make.

    +3 pts

    turnout from same-day registration, according to research the book cites.

    Ninety seconds, then play. The framework is the one idea that recurs in every chapter.

    Chapter 2 in one slide

    Six questions the rules answer

    WHO VOTES · HOW

    Two national limits, fifty sets of rules

    Only citizenship and age 18 apply everywhere. States set registration, ID and mail rules. Absentee voters request a ballot; vote-by-mail sends one to everyone.

    WHO RUNS · WHEN

    Competence, loyalty, a fixed calendar

    Age, citizenship and residency rules reflect competence and loyalty. States may not term-limit Congress. Fixed election dates make the campaign permanent.

    WHERE

    Somebody draws the lines

    Reapportionment moves House seats among states. Redistricting redraws districts to equal populations. Gerrymanders pack or crack the other side's voters.

    WHO WINS

    Plurality rule and Duverger's Law

    Single-member districts plus plurality rule push toward two parties. Maine and Alaska use ranked-choice voting; other democracies use proportional representation.

    36

    states have voter identification laws.

    47

    states plus D.C. allowed early voting as of 2024.

    Chapter 3 in one slide

    Five eras, and less change than you think

    1788–1824Pre-democraticState legislatures pick electors. Candidates barely campaign; other party members do it for them.
    1828–92Mass mobilizationJackson's template: a loyal inner circle, organized voters, entertainment. Turnout reaches 80% in 1840.
    1896–1948ProgressiveCivil service, the Australian ballot and primaries weaken parties. Turnout falls.
    1952–2012CandidateTelevision, primaries and polling. The media market replaces the county.
    2016–nowContemporaryPolarization, sorting, big data and digital outreach.

    Changed

    • Technology: broadcast, then digital and social media
    • The rules: ballots, primaries, finance
    • Who sits in each party's coalition

    Didn't

    • More focus on character than on policy
    • Negative campaigning, since the Federalists
    • Door-to-door work and voter mobilization
    The eras are separated by abrupt changes in rules and reality. The next slide asks teams to rebuild the order.

    Chapter 4 in one slide

    Contributions are limited. Spending is not.

    WHO GIVES

    Individuals and PACs

    Citizens and permanent residents of any age may give. Corporations and unions give only through PACs. Donors of $200 or more are disclosed to the FEC.

    THE LAWS

    Tillman, FECA, BCRA

    Tillman (1907) banned corporate donations. FECA (1971) required disclosure. BCRA (2002) banned party soft money and indexed limits to inflation.

    THE CASES

    From Buckley to Citizens United

    Buckley (1976) allowed contribution limits but not spending limits. Citizens United (2010) freed corporate and union spending. McCutcheon (2014) ended the aggregate limit.

    DOES IT BUY ANYTHING?

    Hard to prove either way

    Trump was outspent in 2016, 2020 and 2024. Donors may back likely winners, and access may matter more than votes.

    $3,500

    per election, what one person may give one candidate in 2025–26.

    $4.2B

    spent by independent groups in 2024.

    The one sentence to leave with: contributions are limited, spending is not.

    Chapter 5 in one slide

    Who a campaign talks to, and who it ignores

    BRADSHAW

    Your base, their base, the undecided

    All three can be estimated. You do not need every vote, so direct resources to the groups that decide the outcome.

    VOTE TARGETS

    Turnout, floor, ceiling

    Past results set a floor and a ceiling. The gap between them is the persuadable vote, and county shares turn the target into local quotas.

    THE INSTRUMENTS

    Samples, focus groups, voter files

    Probability samples measure opinion. Focus groups add depth. Microtargeting needs a poll of about 6,000 matched to the voter file.

    THE DECISIONS

    Whether to run, what to stress, whom to attack

    Strategic politicians run when they can win. Parties own issues. Candidates who are behind are the likeliest to attack.

    80%

    of the persuadable voters a 2028 Republican for North Carolina governor would need, in the book's worked example.

    The next slide is the book's North Carolina arithmetic as a calculator.

    Warm-up · Chapter 6

    Which part of the party is it?

    Sort all eight
    V. O. Key's three parts: the party-in-the-electorate is the voters who identify with it; the party-as-organization is the committees, chairs and staff; the party-in-government is the people holding office under its label. Each is partly independent of the others.

    Chapter 6 in one slide

    Fighting between the 45-yard lines

    THREE PARTS

    Electorate, organization, government

    V. O. Key's three parts of a party. Politicians organized parties before voters called themselves partisans, to win lasting majorities.

    WHY ONLY TWO

    The rules, again

    Single-member districts, plurality rule and winner-take-all electors. Perot won 19% in 1992 and no electoral votes.

    IN CAMPAIGNS

    Recruit, fund, mobilize, signal

    Parties may coordinate with their candidates; super PACs may not. Party contact fell to 21% of citizens in 2024.

    IN DECLINE?

    Mostly no

    Parties are stronger in government and among voters, where most independents are closet partisans. The organization is weaker. Negative partisanship is rising.

    95%

    of House and Senate seats have gone to the two major parties since the 1920s.

    <10%

    of contests have split their results between the parties since 2016.

    The book's line: Democrats and Republicans have been fighting between the 45-yard lines for four decades. No party has won the presidential popular vote by more than 10 points since 1984.

    Chapter 7 in one slide

    Groups want policy, not power

    WHAT THEY ARE

    Shared goals, no candidates

    Business is the largest sector. Public-interest and single-issue groups are likelier to take part in elections, but fewer and poorer.

    THE MONEY

    Giving flat, spending soaring

    Contributions barely moved after 1998 while direct spending reached $3.8 billion in 2024. PAC money goes mostly to incumbents.

    BALLOT INITIATIVES

    Groups replace candidates

    Endorsements work as shortcuts for low-information voters. Group money is better at defeating measures than passing them.

    INFLUENCE

    Real, but smaller than claimed

    Group ads are more negative and more issue-focused. Candidates rarely follow groups' agendas, and leaders overstate the votes they can deliver.

    50

    congressional races in 2024 where outside groups outspent the candidates.

    $15M+

    spent on the 2017 Los Angeles school board race.

    The chapter's opening puzzle: why would the ACLU, which spent almost nothing, matter, while Future Forward, which represents nobody, matters too? The answer is that "the groups" make a convenient target after a loss.

    Chapter 8 in one slide

    The media and candidates want different things

    WHO THEY ARE

    TV still leads

    Print and network audiences shrank; podcasts doubled since 2019. Local news is collapsing, so voters judge everyone by views of the president.

    WHAT GETS COVERED

    Novelty, conflict, the horse race

    In 2016, 42% of coverage was horse race and 10% policy. The average presidential sound bite runs nine seconds.

    IS IT BIASED?

    Toward whoever is ahead

    Content studies find no consistent partisan tilt. Algorithms mostly don't radicalize; users choose. Fox News is the exception that moved votes.

    EFFECTS

    Agenda setting, priming, informing

    Persuasion is hard because most people tune out and partisans reason motivatedly. Effects are strongest in primaries.

    9 sec

    the average presidential sound bite on TV news.

    40%

    of Americans listen to a podcast weekly, double 2019.

    Three themes from the Joe Rogan opener: new formats reach people who catch news only incidentally; traditional outlets still frame the story; media and candidates have incompatible incentives.

    Chapter 9 in one slide

    Two tasks, in tension

    THE NOMINATION

    Rules are strategy

    The invisible primary, delegate rules (a 15% threshold for Democrats) and the calendar. Early states signal viability far beyond their delegates.

    CONVENTIONS · RUNNING MATES

    Small effects

    Bumps averaged about 2 points from 2008 to 2024, and none in 2024. A running mate moves a point or two in a few states at most.

    THE ELECTORAL COLLEGE

    270 of 538

    Winner-take-all is state law. Trump won 58.0% of electoral votes on 49.8% of the popular vote in 2024.

    THE FALL

    Fundamentals first

    The economy matters most; two-thirds of incumbents win. Debates rarely move votes, and coverage of them matters more than the debates.

    98%

    of 2024 presidential broadcast ad money went to the seven battleground states.

    43 + D.C.

    states were targeted only indirectly.

    The chapter's skepticism: campaigns mostly reinforce partisan loyalties and raise the salience of the fundamentals. Correlated with victory is not the same as causing it.

    Chapter 10 in one slide

    Incumbents win, but not for being incumbents

    THE ADVANTAGE

    Shrinking

    The House incumbency advantage peaked just under 10 points in 1986 and fell to 1.2 in 2024. Party loyalty now decides most districts.

    CHALLENGERS

    Quality and money

    Quality challengers have held office before, and they wait for open seats. The average House challenger raised about $467,000 to the incumbent's $3 million.

    RUNNING SCARED

    Rare losses, constant fundraising

    Losing is unlikely but ends a career, so safe incumbents keep raising money. Much of it comes from outside the district.

    NATIONALIZED

    Not all politics is local

    Congressional races now turn on the national parties. Bob Casey's moderate record could not save him in 2024.

    97%

    of House incumbents won reelection in 2024, and 88% of senators.

    67

    of 435 House races were decided by fewer than 10 points.

    The puzzle to leave with: incumbents almost always win, yet the incumbency advantage is close to zero. They win because their districts already lean their way.

    Chapter 11 in one slide

    The races that matter most get the least attention

    NONPARTISAN BALLOTS

    No party cue

    Over 75% of city elections are nonpartisan. Without a party label, turnout falls and voters lean on incumbency.

    OFF-CYCLE ELECTIONS

    Small, organized electorates

    Turnout runs more than 20 points below presidential years. Organized groups such as teachers' unions gain. The book favors moving local elections on-cycle.

    LEGISLATURES AND GOVERNORS

    Few contests

    38% of state legislative candidates had no major-party opponent in 2024. Term limits do not reliably add competition.

    JUDGES AND MEASURES

    Money flows downballot

    Only 8 states elect none of their judges. State supreme court fundraising reached $100 million in 2021–22. Most school bonds pass.

    38%

    of major-party state legislative candidates were unopposed in 2024, against 4% for Congress.

    95%+

    of governors seeking reelection have won since 2020.

    The theme: state and local elections arguably affect daily life the most, and they live up to the ideals of campaigns the least.

    Chapter 12 in one slide

    People vote when someone asks them

    HOW MANY

    64% in 2024

    Midterm turnout usually runs 10 to 20 points lower. Low U.S. turnout comes from the rules: no compulsory voting, frequent elections, a separate registration step.

    WHO

    Ability, motivation, opportunity

    Political interest is the strongest individual factor. Education builds ability; registration rules shape opportunity.

    WHAT WORKS

    Face to face

    Canvassing gains the most votes per contact. Campaign mail, email and TV ads do little. Social pressure works.

    THE CATCH

    Mobilization can widen gaps

    Likely voters are the easiest to mobilize, so get-out-the-vote drives can widen participation gaps rather than close them.

    48%

    of 18- to 24-year-olds voted in 2024, against 75% of those 65 and older.

    15–19 pts

    of turnout from enforced compulsory voting.

    Competitive races raise turnout because more people are asked to vote, not because voters think their ballot will decide the race.

    Chapter 13 in one slide

    Do campaigns matter?

    PARTY

    The strongest predictor

    Over 90% of partisans vote for their party's nominee. Only about 7% are pure independents. Dislike of the other party has grown.

    FUNDAMENTALS

    The economy and the incumbent

    Models in August 2024 forecast 50.2% for Trump; he got 50.8%. Independents' votes respond most to the economy.

    ISSUES AND GROUPS

    Fewer issue voters than you think

    Voters often adopt their candidate's positions. Groups shape votes through contact with other members.

    WHEN CAMPAIGNS MATTER

    Primaries and mismatches

    Campaigns matter most with unfamiliar candidates or lopsided resources. General elections usually fight to a draw.

    5 of 14

    presidential elections from 1948 to 2000 appear to have been decided by the campaign.

    <230,000

    votes across Michigan, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin decided 2024.

    The answer to the chapter's question is "sometimes." Campaigns usually reinforce and prime the fundamentals, but close races can turn on them.
